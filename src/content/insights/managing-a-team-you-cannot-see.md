Most diaspora business owners we talk to don't think of themselves as having an HR problem. They think of themselves as having a "staff issue," singular, usually attached to one person's name. The delivery lead who keeps missing targets. The office manager who's brilliant but impossible to reach on Fridays. The hire from eight months ago nobody's checked in on since onboarding.

Individually, each of those looks like a performance problem. Together, they're almost always the same structural problem wearing different faces: nobody owns people management as a job. It's distributed across a WhatsApp group, a handful of voice notes, and whatever time the owner has left after actually running the business from another country.

That gap is invisible until it costs you something. A resignation with no notice. A dispute that should have been resolved with a documented policy and instead becomes a shouting match at 11pm your time. A good hire who leaves after a year because nobody ever gave them a real onboarding, a clear reporting line, or a performance conversation that wasn't a crisis.

## The three symptoms worth checking for

**No one owns the relationship.** If you asked "who is this person's manager" about every member of your African team, would the answer be a name, or would it be "technically me, but really no one"? If it's the second answer for more than one person, you have a structure problem, not a personnel problem.

**Policy lives in memory, not in writing.** Ask yourself what your leave policy actually is. Not what you'd like it to be, what it actually is, in writing, that a new hire could read on day one. If the honest answer is "it depends," that's a liability waiting for the wrong moment to surface.

**Escalation only happens at crisis point.** If the first time you hear about a problem is when it's already a resignation letter or a dispute, you don't have a reporting structure. You have a smoke alarm that only goes off after the fire's spread.

## What actually fixes it

Not more of your own time. That's the instinct, and it's the wrong one, because the constraint was never effort, it was presence. You can't be in two time zones at once, and trying to compensate with more calls and more check-ins just adds hours to a system that's still structurally broken.

What fixes it is putting someone specific, senior enough to make calls without escalating everything, formally responsible for the people side of your business, in a way that's aligned to your working hours rather than the other side of the world's. That's not a hire you're likely to make for one role. It's a function you bring in.

The businesses that get this right treat people management the way they'd treat finance: as a specific competency that needs a specific owner, not a task that gets absorbed into whoever has a spare hour. The ones that don't tend to keep hiring well and losing well, at the same rate, indefinitely.

If any part of this sounds familiar, it's worth a conversation before the next resignation letter, not after.
